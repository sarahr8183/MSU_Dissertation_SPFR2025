\section{Diamond in Quantum Technology}
\label{quantumtech}
\indent
	
	Aside from diamond's status in the jewelry industry, the physical properties of diamond have driven its popularity in a wide range of applications. Diamond is mechanically hard, resistant to chemical corrosion, biologically compatible, an ultra-wide bandgap semiconductor \cite{PaulM2000}, and has high carrier mobilities \cite{Isberg2002} making it suitable for polishing hard materials like stainless steel \cite{Ranjan2019}, targeted drug delivery \cite{Man2012}, high power electronic devices such as Schottky diodes \cite{Nicley2015}, and quantum technologies \cite{Greentree2008}. Quantum applications of diamond cover a wide range from magnetic field sensing \cite{Maze2008} to communications \cite{Lin2019} and quantum computing \cite{Weber2010} making quantum diamond materials research a field of high impact.

	There are several reason diamond is particularly suitable for quantum applications. First, as an ultra-wide bandgap semiconductor with a bandgap approximately five times larger than silicon's, diamond is known to host thousands of different defects without the electronic structures of those defects interfering with the electronic structure of the diamond host material \cite{Weber2010}. Second, carbon-12 has a relative abundance of 98.89 \% and carbon-12 isotope enriched samples and sources can be used to further improve the isotopic purity of synthetic diamond. Carbon-12 has zero nuclear spin which is important for quantum applications as nuclear spin is a source of decoherence \cite{Man2012}. These two properties allow the defects in diamond to exist as isolated atoms in what is, essentially, a spin-free bath. The nuclear spin of carbon-13 can also be useful; some researchers have had success in using the nuclear spin of carbon-13 as a register for qubit states acting as a quantum memory for the diamond qubit \cite{Bradley2019AMinute}.

	Diamond defects are also called color centers because the electronic structure of the defects can emit and absorb visible light giving color to an otherwise transparent host diamond. A few of the more widely studied color centers include: nitrogen vacancy defect (NV) \cite{Jelezko2001SpectroscopyDiamond, Gruber1997ScanningCenters}, silicon vacancy defect (SiV) \cite{Goss1996TheComplex,Clark1994SiliconDiamond}, tin vacancy defect (SnV)\cite{Iwasaki2017Tin-VacancyDiamond}, and the nickel vacancy defect (NiV)\cite{Nazare1991OpticalDiamond, Mason1999MagneticDiamond}. Slight differences in defects make them more or less suitable for different applications. Some success in using the nitrogen vacancy defect for quantum communication has been realized, however, the NV center has a zero phonon line emission at 637 nm which is sub-optimal since the compatible wavelength range for maximum transmission of optical fibers is 1300 - 1550 nm \cite{Hermans2022QubitNetwork,Hensen2015Loophole-freeKilometres}.  Additionally, NV centers have a low Debye-Waller factor of 0.03 \cite{Kehayias2013InfraredDiamond}. This means only three percent of the total emission from NV centers is from the zero phonon line (ZPL) which is where coherent photons are generated \cite{Thiering2020ColorApplications}. Coherent photons are necessary for most quantum applications \cite{Thiering2020ColorApplications}. While other defects, with high Debye-Waller factors,  are better suited for quantum computing and quantum communication, the nitrogen vacancy center is best suited for quantum sensing applications \cite{Achard2020}. NV defects have long spin coherence times because they are a spin-1 system which means they do not couple to the spin-1/2 bath, i.e. electrons,  in the host material. Long coherence times are crucial for quantum sensing applications. Thus, the NV defect is a strong candidate for quantum sensing.

\section{The Nitrogen Vacancy Defect as a Quantum Sensor}
\label{NVintro}
\indent
	
	The negatively charged nitrogen vacancy defect (NV$^{-}$) is comprised of a single substitutional nitrogen on a carbon lattice site with a vacancy on an adjacent lattice site. The negative indicates the defect has trapped an additional electron \cite{GDavies1976}. The energy diagram for the NV$^{-}$ defect is shown in Figure \ref{fig: NVEnergyLevels} \cite{Mzyk2022}. There are several important properties of the NV$^{-}$ center that make it well suited for quantum sensing applications \cite{Weber2010}. First, the quantum state can be polarized via optical pumping. In Figure \ref{fig: NVEnergyLevels}, the metastable intersystem crossing polarizes the state to the m$_s$=0 state because there is a strong spin-orbit coupling with the excited m$_s$=$\pm$1 state and the intersystem crossing but a weak spin-orbit coupling with the excited m$_s$=0 state and the intersystem crossing. Over time, this results in a higher probability that electrons will be in the m$_s$=0 state. Because of the quantum state polarization resulting in higher probabilities that electrons will be in the m$_s$=0 state, the m$_s$=0 state presents brighter than the state m$_s$=$\pm$1 giving a luminescent distinction between states. Lastly, Figure \ref{fig: NVEnergyLevels} shows the Zeeman Effect in the NV$^-$ center. When a magnetic field is applied, the separation between the m$_s$=$+$1 and m$_s$=$-$1 dark states increases proportionally to the strength of the field. This property of the NV$^-$ center is one way it can be used to sense magnetic fields. NV$^-$ centers can also detect magnetic fields through phase accumulation. Here, the state of the NV$^-$ defect is projected onto the x-y plane of the Bloch sphere where it evolves in time until the state is read out. The long coherence times of NV$^-$ defects allows the phase information to be preserved while in the x-y plane of the Bloch sphere.

    To more broadly utilize the nitrogen vacancy defect for quantum sensing applications, controllable and repeatable manufacturing of NV$^-$ centers in high-quality SCD is necessary. 
    
\subsection{Nitrogen Vacancy Defect}
\label{NVFab}
\indent 
This section will discuss current methods for creating nitrogen vacancy defects in diamond starting with methods for growing synthetic diamond. Then, methods for introducing nitrogen into the diamond lattice and forming vacancies in the diamond lattice will be discussed followed by a discussion of diamond annealing to create the nitrogen vacancy defects.

\subsubsection{Synthetic Diamond}
\label{syntheicDiamond}
\indent
    
    The first step to repeatable and controllable manufacturing of NV$^-$ defects is repeatable manufacturing of diamond. Diamond is an allotrope of carbon that is not thermodynamically favorable at standard temperature and pressure; under these conditions, the thermodynamically favorable allotrope is graphite \cite{PaulM2000}. Diamond is naturally produced in the Earth’s mantle, because the mantle provides the high pressure high temperature environment necessary to achieve thermodynamic stability. Diamond then travels to the surface where it can be mined for jewelry, for example. Diamond can also be synthetically grown using high pressure high temperature (HPHT) techniques or chemical vapor deposition (CVD). Synthetic diamond properties can be more repeatable than natural diamonds because of the control over growth conditions and ability to monitor key reaction parameters. 

    \noindent\textbf{High Pressure High Temperature Diamond}
    \newline\indent
     High pressure high temperature (HPHT) was the first successful technique for synthetic diamond growth \cite{Bundy1955Man-MadeDiamonds}. The HPHT technique achieves thermodynamically favorable conditions for the diamond allotrope of carbon. Here, a carbon containing material, such as graphite, is combined with molten metal in a reactor under high pressure, high temperature conditions matching those in the Earth’s mantle \cite{Bovenkerk1993}. After several days to months, the reactor is then decompressed and the diamond sample is removed. This technique can produce large single crystal diamonds, several millimeters in size \cite{Sumiya1996High-pressureCrystal}. Dopants can be incorporated into HPHT grown diamond samples by including desired species in the reactor cell. HPHT synthetic diamond from Sumitomo, for example, can contain several hundreds ppm nitrogen. The HPHT technique, however, does not allow \textit{in situ} control of dopant incorporation.

    \noindent\textbf{Chemical Vapor Deposition}
    \newline\indent
    The second technique to synthetically produce diamond, chemical vapor depositions (CVD), was demonstrated in 1968 \cite{Angus1968GrowthDeposition}. CVD reactors exist in many different designs including hot filament or microwave plasma assisted designs which differ in their method for carbon radical production \cite{PaulM2000}. Additionally, two different geometries exist for CVD reactors include bell jar and tube flow reactors where each design fills a certain niche for the CVD process \cite{Dahmen2003}. The CVD technique exploits the kinetic aspect of the reaction by focusing on adding one gaseous carbon radical at a time to an existing seed crystal to grow the single crystal diamond (SCD) \cite{PaulM2000}. The seed crystal can be diamond (homoepitaxy) or a different material (heteroepitaxy). Homoepitaxial growth results in less strain in the grown film. To minimize the strain introduced into heteroepitaxial grown films, the seed crystal should have a similar lattice parameter and crystal structure. In homoepitaxial CVD, a gaseous carbon source such as methane in a predominately hydrogen gas phase attaches to the dangling \textit{sp$^3$} hybridized carbon atom in the diamond lattice at the surface of the seed crystal \cite{PaulM2000,Harris1990MechanismRadicals}. It is crucial to have a predominately hydrogen gas phase because hydrogen favorably etches graphitic carbon (\textit{sp$^2$} hybridization) thus leaving the hybridized carbons that favor the diamond lattice  \cite{PaulM2000}. Figure \ref{fig: CVDSchematic} provides a graphical representation of the carbon radicals attaching to the dangling bonds that were exposed by hydrogen radical. This technique yields a sort of layer-by-layer approach as opposed to the bulk transition to SCD that occurs in the HPHT technique.

    The more incremental approach to diamond growth via CVD, as opposed to the more bulk process of HPHT grown SCD, facilitates \textit{in situ} process changes to control the final product. For example, the gas phase composition could be altered for some amount of time and then returned to its initial composition resulting in a layered structure. This was demonstrated by Lobaev \textit{et al.} for nitrogen in a laminar flow reactor \cite{Lobaev2017}. For this reason, CVD is a promising technique for spatial control of nitrogen incorporated in growing synthetic SCD; a crucial aspect of repeatable and controllable manufacturing of NV centers in diamond. CVD of synthetic diamond will be discussed in more detail in Chapter \ref{chDiamondGrowth}.

\subsubsection{Nitrogen Incorporation in Diamond Lattice}
\label{Nincorp}
\indent
    The necessary steps to fabricate NV$^-$ defects are nitrogen incorporation, vacancy formation, and NV center creation. Nitrogen can be incorporated post growth process via ion implantation or \textit{in situ} during synthetic diamond growth. Nitrogen is a common defect in diamond due to its natural abundance. Nitrogen incorporation in synthetic diamond growth is anisotropic. During HPHT synthetic diamond growth, nitrogen preferentially incorporates in the \{111\} family of planes, then the \{100\}, \{113\}, and lastly \{110\} \cite{Burns1990GROWTH-SECTORDIAMONDS}. Nitrogen incorporation in CVD diamond growth differs slightly. In synthetic CVD diamond growth, nitrogen preferentially incorporates in the \{111\} family of planes, just as in HPHT, however, the \{100\} family of planes has the lowest probability of nitrogen incorporation \cite{Samlenski1995IncorporationDiamond}. Intentional nitrogen incorporation via ion implantation and \textit{in situ} doping will be discussed in more detail below.


\noindent\textbf{Ion Implantation}
 \newline\indent 
 For ion implantation to fabricate NV$^-$ defects, nitrogen ions are accelerated towards a diamond substrate where they bombard the surface at a set fluence (electrons/cm$^2$). The high energy nitrogen atoms enter the diamond substrate and knock carbon atoms off lattice sites, creating some vacancies, or fill interstitial sites. The depth nitrogen ions penetrate into the diamond substrate is dependent on the accelerating voltage whereas the quantity of nitrogen is dependent on fluence and time of implantation. Nanoaperture masks have been used to implement spatial control of the ion implantation process \cite{Spinicelli2011EngineeredNanoapertures}. The ion implantation technique introduces lattice damage and inhomogeneous strain.

\noindent\textbf{\textit{In situ} Doping}
    \newline \indent
    An alternative to ion implantation is \textit{in situ} doping via CVD or HPHT. For HPHT diamond synthesis, nitrogen can be intentionally added or getters can be selected such that some nitrogen remains. For CVD, nitrogen is introduced into the gas phase of the CVD reactor. The nitrogen can then incorporate at the \textit{sp$^3$} dangling bonds, as shown in Figure \ref{fig: CVDSchematic}, creating single substitutional nitrogen defects (P1 centers). Depth control of single substitutional nitrogen using \textit{in situ} doping in CVD can be done by introducing and removing the nitrogen in the gas phase at set times. 
	

\subsubsection{Vacancy Formation in Diamond Lattice}
\label{FormVac}
\indent
	The next step in creating NV$^-$ defects is to form vacancies. Vacancies can be introduced through irradiation processes. If ion implantation was used to incorporate nitrogen, some vacancies form during the process and further vacancy formation through irradiation may not be necessary. 
    
\noindent\textbf{Electron Beam Irradiation}
\newline \indent
    Electron beam irradiation is similar to ion implantation in that the sample is bombarded. However, electron beam irradiation bombards the surface with electrons as opposed to ions \cite{Luo2021}. Electron beam irradiation is commonly used to create vacancies in diamond. Vacancy formation is not inherent to \textit{in situ} CVD doping as it is with ion implantation. Therefore, electron beam irradiation is used to create vacancies in doped diamond. Accelerating voltage, fluence, and irradiation time determine depth and concentration of vacancies. Spatial control of vacancy formation by transmission electron microscope (TEM) electron beam irradiation has been demonstrated \cite{McLellan2016PatternedTechnique}.
       
\noindent\textbf{Laser Writing}
\newline \indent
    A more recently developed technique known as laser writing is another way to create vacancies in the diamond lattice. In this technique, Chen \textit{et al.} demonstrated a localized irradiation and annealing process with \textit{in situ} characterization to create isolated single NV$^-$ defects \cite{Chen2017LaserDiamond}. Here, an HPHT diamond with approximately 1.8 ppm of single substitutional nitrogen defects was irradiated with a laser pulse to create an ensemble of vacancies in a region where no NV centers were previously observed. The vacancy formation step was immediately followed by irradiation with lower energy pulses to locally anneal \cite{Chen2017LaserDiamond}. During annealing, the fluorescence of the region was monitored so that once an NV$^-$ center was detected, the pulses are stopped, thus creating a single NV$^-$ center \cite{Chen2017LaserDiamond}. This technique requires a low NV$^-$ background in order to detect the creation of a single NV$^-$ center. For example, if single NV$^-$ produces a count rate of 20 kHz in a given system, then it would be difficult to distinguish a single NV$^-$ in a background of greater than 200 kHz. Several factors contribute the count rates of a single NV$^-$ in a given system such as excited state lifetime and charge state stability in the sample as well as collection efficiency and laser power of the optical system\cite{Kirkpatrick2023OpticalDiamond}.

    The laser writing technique offers near unity NV$^-$ creation yield with positioning accuracy in the x and y of less than 50 nm \cite{Chen2019}. The positioning in the z direction is less precise at approximately 200 nm due to the Lorentzian distribution in along the z-axis, a limiting factor in this process \cite{Chen2019}.
    
    
\subsubsection{Diamond Annealing}
\label{annealing}
    Typically, an annealing step will follow ion implantation or other vacancy formation techniques. Annealing is a diffusive process that provides atoms within a sample the thermal energy needed to obtain a lower stress. The annealing process promotes diffusion of the nitrogen and/or vacancy species. It is known that vacancies tend to settle adjacent to single substitutional nitrogen centers \cite{Savage2021}. Additionally, the annealing process reduces the free energy of the system. This is crucial because the implantation and irradiation processes introduce damage and inhomogeneous strain to the diamond lattice  both of which are detrimental to the operation of the NV$^-$ defect in quantum sensing applications.
    
    One example of electron irradiation and annealing being used to form NV defects was completed by Luo \textit{et al.} \cite{Luo2021}. Here, four CVD substrates were electron irradiated and annealed. The absorption spectra of the four samples were characterized after each step and compared. Figures \ref{fig: AbsAfterIrr} and \ref{fig: AbsAfterIrrAndAnneal} show absorption spectra pre- and post- annealing, respectively, of four samples irradiated at the same electron energy but different fluence \cite{Luo2021}. All four samples were CVD grown under the same conditions from CVD seeds that were laser cut and removed to prevent contribution to the spectra from the seed \cite{Luo2021}. A spectrum of the as-grown sample was used as a background and subtracted from each spectrum to show the change in defects due to the irradiation or annealing process. The peak at approximately 393 nm is a result of the negatively charged vacancy (V$^-$) defect \cite{Luo2021}. The intensity of the peak is proportional to the concentration of the defect. Therefore, Figure \ref{fig: AbsAfterIrr} demonstrates an increase in V$^-$ concentrations as the fluence increases \cite{Luo2021}. Additionally, a peak at approximately 741 nm within a broad side band from approximately 500-750 nm, which is attributed to the neutral vacancy defect (V$^0$), emerges after irradiation and persists after annealing for the highest fluence sample, shown in Figure \ref{fig: AbsAfterIrr} \cite{Luo2021}. Both observations are logical because increasing fluence means more electrons per unit area are hitting the sample and knocking atoms off lattice sites. Because these samples are being irradiated with an electron beam, it is also logical that the V$^-$ occurs at higher concentration than the V$^0$ because the increase in number of electrons bombarding the sample introduces more electrons into the sample for a vacancy to potentially trap.

	Figure \ref{fig: AbsAfterIrrAndAnneal} shows the same four samples after annealing \cite{Luo2021}. The NV$^-$ peak occurs at 637 nm and is accompanied by a broad phonon side band extending to approximately 450 nm \cite{Luo2021}; in both spectroscopic features, intensity increases as the fluence increases. The NV$^0$ peak occurs at 575 nm, within the broad phonon side band of the NV$^-$. However, the NV$^0$ peak becomes visible for the sample with 1$\times$10$^{18}$ e/cm$^2$ and 3$\times$10$^{18}$ e/cm$^2$ fluence \cite{Luo2021}. Figure \ref{fig: AbsAfterIrrAndAnneal} also indicates an excess of vacancies that did not form NV$^{-/0}$ centers because the 741 nm and 393 nm peaks are still visible, more notably at high fluence \cite{Luo2021}. High fluence is expected to produce more vacancies, due to the higher flux of the electrons, and vacancies do not always find a P1 defect during annealing. In any case, the concentration of NV$^-$ defects within the samples increased through the irradiation and annealing processes. The shortcoming of this process is, again, the lack of spatial control of the NV$^-$ center formation. 

    The laser writing technique offers a method for spatially controlled creating of NV$^-$ defects through localized irradiation and annealing. This process also offers \textit{in situ} characterization that near unity formation of single NV$^-$ defects \cite{Chen2019}.
	

\subsection{Current Limitations}
\indent

As shown in the discussions above, there are many ways to create NV$^-$ defects in diamond. There are pros and cons to each technique. Synthetic diamond growth via the HPHT technique allows for large SCD growths but does not offer the \textit{in situ} dopant control that CVD synthetic diamond growth offers. Large diamonds are not needed for diamond-based quantum sensors. For this reason, the CVD synthetic diamond growth technique is the preferred synthetic growth technique for the application proposed in this dissertation. CVD diamond growth offer \textit{in situ} control over dopant incorporation. Dopants can be incorporated post growth via techniques like ion implantation, however, ion implantation introduces damage to the diamond and lacks the ability to spatially control dopants. Annealing is essential after ion implantation in order to reduce the damage done during implantation. Annealing promotes the lowest energy defect configuration which, as discussed in Section \ref{annealing}, is the NV$^-$ defect. Ion implantation and annealing or electron beam irradiation after doping forms ensembles of NV$^-$ defects as opposed to single NV$^-$ centers. Therefore, these techniques are better suited for diamond-based quantum sensing aimed at improving sensitivity with ensembles of NV$^-$ defect rather than quantum sensing aimed at improving spatial resolution with single NV$^-$ defects. This further solidifies CVD diamond growth as the preferred method for synthetic diamond growth and nitrogen incorporation with the use of laser writing for spatial control of single NV$^-$ defect formation.  
       
Though the laser writing technique shows promise for placement of NV$^-$ in the x and y direction, the z direction control is unfavorable \cite{Chen2019}. The significant variation in depth of nitrogen vacancy centers affects the coherence time and signal strength. Further improvements in depth control of nitrogen vacancy center placement is necessary to further improve high spatial resolution quantum sensing with NV$^-$ centers.
